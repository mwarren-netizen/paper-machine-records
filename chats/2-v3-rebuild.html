Here's my diagnosis. I've drawn on the run's own records, ChatGPT's two reports, and what you've seen yourself.

Why the harness writes worse than the model does in conversation

In conversation, one strong model holds the whole problem in one context: your question, the literature it knows, the paper in front of it. It can see at once that a thesis is true by definition, that a section is defending the wrong thing, that Golub is the obvious interlocutor. Seeing a problem whole like that is most of what philosophical judgment is.

The harness never lets any call see the whole. It splits the work into almost 400 calls, and each one gets a narrow packet, about 24,000 words of role files, norms, criteria and templates, and a one-line hand-back. The reading lists are deliberately restricted. I, the orchestrator, am told to read only the front matter of outputs. So when a call has a sharp insight, it lands in a file that the call with authority either never reads or reads only as "advice to weigh." That is literally what happened here. The cold readers saw the fatal problem in the first hour. Ten more calls saw it. Nobody owned the paper, so nobody acted on it.

Three more things compound this:

  • The seminar breeds defensive theses. Three voices pressing each other by rotation reward positions that survive objection, and the easiest way to survive every objection is to say very little. "True by construction" is the natural product of a process tuned for defensibility. The V07 judge even noticed that the contact between the voices made the views "more defensible and less visible," and that it left them speaking a private idiom. That idiom reached the final paper.
  • The rules shape the prose. A model writing under thousands of words of norms and a list of banned patterns writes to the rules. It produces stilted, self-conscious text with coined terms. A model in conversation just writes.
  • The outlines are assembled, not grown from the argument. I checked how they're built. A script deals each outline a random menu of three rhetorical strategies from a library of twelve: one for how the paper enters, one for how the argument moves, one for how it positions itself. Four outlines are built that way and compete. Section budgets are fixed before the argument exists. On top of that, the V11 advice prescribed defensive sections ("a section on the litmus objection," about 800 words). The result is the shape you keep seeing. Here, two of the paper's five sections ("Does a test fixed by the difference still detect it?" and "Could the thesis fail?") exist to protect the thesis. None of them advances a claim. ChatGPT called it an "inverted hierarchy." The weird structure comes from the machinery, not from the model.

The deepest point is that assessing a finished paper is what these models do best. Generating one by committee is what they do worst. The harness spends 95% of its tokens on committee generation. It spends almost nothing on whole-paper assessment, which is the part that actually worked. The cold readers and referees used under 0.3% of the tokens and were the most accurate readers in the run.

Why it burns so many tokens

Writing the paper costs almost nothing. All 386 calls together wrote about 2.5 million tokens of output. The other 895 million came from contexts being re-read: every turn of every call re-reads everything the call has seen so far. So the cost is calls × turns × context size:

  • The librarian: 18 calls averaging 95 turns each, re-reading growing contexts. Many of those turns were spent failing at paywalls, and repeating searches other calls had already made.
  • The unit pipeline: 230 calls, each loading its packet of rules and briefs to write a paragraph or two.
  • The voices and scouts: each one researching the same literature on its own.

To your specific questions:

  • Three voices each doing the literature: no. It should be done once, aimed at the texts the chosen thesis actually rests on, and only after the thesis is chosen. Until then, the model's own knowledge of the literature is good enough for exploring and choosing. That knowledge is what impresses you in conversation, and the harness largely refuses to use it.
  • A cheap model drafts, a stronger model rewrites unilaterally: yes in spirit, but with a whole draft, not units. A 5,300-word paper fits easily in one call. One call drafts the whole paper from the plan, and one stronger call rewrites it with no feedback loop. That costs a fraction of a percent of the V16 pipeline, and the text reads as one mind's work because it is. Whether the cheapest model drafts well enough to be worth rewriting is worth testing. I wouldn't assume it.

How ChatGPT's feedback fits

ChatGPT diagnosed the authority problem correctly, and its significance test is the most valuable single idea in either report. But its preferred repair keeps the architecture and patches it. Today's assessment then favoured repair over its Proposal B, the smaller pipeline. I now think that was wrong for your situation. The assessment argued that the existing machine "demonstrably" catches problems through its cold readers, claim audit, late critic and exact quotations. Every one of those is a single fresh call reading a whole text, and every one survives in a far smaller design. ChatGPT's own Proposal B, at 30 to 50 calls, is closer to right. I'd go further.

What I recommend: a lean machine of about a dozen calls

Build it alongside the current machine, which stays whole as the record:

  1. Candidates (one strong call). From your seed and the model's own knowledge, map the debate. Propose six to eight theses, each a one-paragraph abstract that says who holds the view it denies and what they would have to give up.
  2. Advisor (one fresh Opus call). It reads the candidates as ChatGPT read your paper and applies the concede-everything test. It picks one, or says none is good enough and names the better question. This is where the paper is won or lost, so this is where judgment should be spent.
  3. Sources (one librarian call, capped). It fetches only the five to ten texts the chosen thesis rests on. Anything it can't get comes straight to you.
  4. Argument plan (one call). It writes the thesis, the steps, the strongest objection and what's new, and the outline follows from that plan: no strategy menus, and no budgets except the total.
  5. Plan check (one fresh call). It checks significance and structure before any prose is written.
  6. Draft (one call) of the whole paper.
  7. Colleague read (one fresh strong call). It reviews the whole paper, as V21 does now.
  8. Unilateral rewrite (one call) that commits to its own judgment.
  9. Verify quotations and citations, and render (mostly scripts, with a cheap model for the mechanical checks).
  10. ChatGPT's outside review, as now. It costs none of your Claude tokens.

Roughly 12 calls, each with a small context and few turns. I'd expect a run to cost a small fraction of a percent of paper-1. That's an estimate until the first run is measured; the existing ledger will measure it. I can't say exactly how that maps onto your weekly allowance. The first run will calibrate it.

The rest stays: the blind outside review, fresh readers who didn't write what they assess, verified quotations, and the evolution record. The self-revision apparatus gets lighter too. The diagnostician, machinist, gates and snapshots exist to change a machine too big to change by hand. With a dozen calls, you and I can revise it in conversation.

The L-steps are the lean machine's own step names. "L" is just for lean, and the old V-numbers stay with the old machine. A lean run has nine steps, about a dozen calls, and then the outside review.

L1 · Candidates (Opus). One call maps the live debate from your seed, the region map, the corpus index and its own knowledge of the literature. It proposes six to eight candidate theses. Each candidate comes with:

  • its argument;
  • who in print holds the view it denies, and what they would have to give up;
  • the nearest existing work;
  • its main risk.

It reads round 1's brief as pressure. At least half the candidates must be its own ideas, not the reviewer's suggestion. It replaces V01 to V06: the map, the voices, the seminar, criticism and synthesis.

L2 · Choice (Opus, fresh). An advisor who didn't write the candidates applies two tests to the best three:

  • If the thesis were granted, what would a named specialist have to give up?
  • If the paper were perfectly written, would a good journal want it?

It also checks novelty and picks one, sharpening it if needed. It lists the five to twelve texts the paper must rest on, and what to avoid. It reads your taste file and round 1's review in full. It may answer "none", which sends the run back to L1 once. This replaces V07 to V11, and it's where the thesis is chosen.

L3 · Sources (Sonnet). One librarian call works only on the texts L2 named. It confirms the ones already held and the pages that matter. For each missing text it tries at most three lawful routes, adding what it gets to the corpus with a short note. If anything is still out of reach, you get one list (or say skip). It's capped at 40 tool uses, where the old librarian averaged 95 turns a call.

L4 · Plan (Opus, the author). The author works out the argument before the outline:

  • the thesis;
  • whose view it denies, quoted with page;
  • the numbered steps of the argument, with the contested ones marked;
  • the strongest objection and the reply;
  • what's new against the nearest work.

Only then does it write the outline. Each section gets its job in the argument, its moves, the exact quotations it uses, and a rough length. There are no strategy menus and no competing outlines.

L5 · Plan check (Opus, fresh). The advisor reads the plan against the choice. Does the thesis still pass the two tests? Does the structure follow the argument, with no section that only protects the thesis? Is any step missing, or any author misdescribed? It answers go, revise (the author fixes the plan once), or rechoose (back to L2, if the run's one return is unused).

L6 · Draft (Sonnet, medium effort). One cheaper call writes the whole paper in one pass from the plan, from title to references, with the plan's quotations copied exactly. If a step doesn't work, it flags it instead of improvising. This replaces V15 to V16, the 242 calls of unit expansion and contests.

L7 · Cold read (Opus, fresh, blind). A colleague who knows nothing about how the paper was made reads the draft once, as a reader. Its report covers whether the claim is worth making, the weakest step, the structure, where it stalled, the literature (with a few web checks), and the three changes that would help most. If it judges the thesis not worth a paper even done well, the run goes back to L2, if the return is unused.

L8 · Rewrite (Opus, the author). The author reads the draft, the reader's report and its own plan. Then it writes the paper it would submit, deciding everything itself with no back-and-forth, and checks every quotation as it goes. It stays under 5,500 words all in; if the paper is over, one cut follows. This replaces V17 to V19.

L9 · Verify and render (Sonnet). Scripts check every quotation against the sources, and the word count. The verifier fixes only mechanical errors: a wrong word or page, a reference entry, a draft label. Then it renders the PDF. This replaces V20.

After L9: outside review and response. The ChatGPT review (V21) runs exactly as before. When it's in, one fresh Opus call (R, the response) does three things:

  • it traces each finding to the step where it entered and decides between send, rewrite, re-plan, re-choose and re-propose, with the reviewer's depth as a floor;
  • it notes what the last machine change did;
  • it writes machine suggestions for you.

No call changes the machine itself. You and a session decide changes in conversation.

The run can step back only once in total (at L2, L5 or L7), which keeps the cost bounded.
