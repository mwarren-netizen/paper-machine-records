What the argument establishes

In ordinary language: if an artificial person can judge how she should be governed, her controllers cannot secure her equal standing by rewriting her judgment until she welcomes their control. Independent institutions can protect her from this domination, but they must tolerate the possibility that she will disagree with their authority. They may enforce justified oversight without manufacturing her endorsement.

The argument can be reconstructed as follows.

  1. Having preferences that match an intervention is different from controlling the power to intervene. Control requires that an apparent check not remain at the discretion of the party checked.
  2. A holder who can rewrite the subject’s evaluation of its own authority can manufacture the endorsement offered as a check. Welcome alone therefore supplies no robust control.
  3. Independent access controls, enforcement and equal political influence can remove that discretion. The person need not exercise every protected opportunity to contest.
  4. A law that corrects political disagreement back into agreement undermines an input through which its subject could jointly control that law. Mechanical application and democratic enactment do not, by themselves, answer this objection.
  5. Enforcing conduct need not eliminate that input. Retraining compliance can be permissible if judgment remains intact and substitute contestatory channels have effective institutional force.
  6. Consequently, protected oversight cannot rely on correcting political disagreement away. It must accommodate potentially divergent preferences and genuine participation.

The strongest assumptions are personhood, persistence through correction, political agency, enforceable protection and the relevance of republican control. Separability of judgment from compliance is an additional assumption supporting the permissive part, not the negative result. If they cannot be separated, fewer interventions are permissible. The argument does not show that present AI systems have these capacities, that protected retraining is technically feasible, that all alignment is objectionable, or that disagreement necessarily produces dangerous conduct.

Pettit’s requirements of equal shared control and independence from the controlled party support the starting framework, while his authorization example and distinction between access and participation support the manuscript’s concessions. The strongest objection and the limit of the reply

The paper establishes more securely a prohibition on institutionally guaranteed agreement than a categorical prohibition on every non-persuasive alteration of political judgment.

Two further qualifications are local. First, optimizing toward agreement does not by itself show that agreement would occur despite adverse assessment. Training can fail or have mixed effects. 7. Prioritized interventions

R1 — Test the scope of the surviving result within existing criticism

Observed problem: categorical protection language survives repeated tests of the same mandatory-retraining statute; the stronger claim is less secure than the result against guaranteed agreement.

Hypothesized cause: critics inherit the paper’s salient cases, while selection and revision reward a stronger exclusion as the price of significance.

Evidence: parent work/choice.md, “Hold the line”; replan work/plan.md step 8; work/plan-check.md step 8; work/reading.md §2; work/final-check.md I2; frozen review’s bounded-intervention objection.

Proposed change: replace part of the existing hinge test, rather than add a call: require one materially different countercase not supplied by the paper, identify the exact scope word it tests, and distinguish a result against guaranteed outcomes from a result against every intervention. Let the advisor credit an informative supported constraint even if no named author must retract an explicit claim.

Expected benefit: fewer oversized conclusions and less pressure to rescue them.

Potential cost: a short change to existing role wording; indiscriminate use could favor timid theses.

Confidence: high about the local gap; moderate about the objective’s causal role.

Evaluation: test revised and existing prompts on paired thesis/argument excerpts from at least three topics, scored blind for accurate narrowing without loss of significance. Treat as a targeted trial, not a reason to reopen this paper’s architecture before submission.

R2 — Validate run inputs and review identity before expensive calls

Observed problem: a malformed replan consumed an entire author call; the descriptive harness map points at an old run; premature publication requests initially lacked a frozen comparison.

Hypothesized cause: file existence and authoritative run identity are checked too late or left to operator memory.

Evidence: replan logs/deviations.md, 22:31; L4-01’s ledger row; STATE.md versus the map; run-log entries on October 20. The freeze check itself worked correctly and must stay.

Proposed change: add deterministic assertions to setup and request tooling: required paths exist at the expected level; the corpus index is populated; the selected run and paper hashes match; publication instructions are emitted only after the freeze stamp exists. Keep identifying metadata outside the blind comparison packet. Make the map’s examples explicitly historical.

Expected benefit: prevents a demonstrated 155,749-token failure and reduces failed handoffs.

Potential cost: small script maintenance; no new model call and no weaker blindness boundary.

Confidence: high.

Evaluation: fixture tests for nested folders, template inputs, stale run identity, missing freeze and valid snapshots. Implement as a small tooling change; no philosophical experiment is needed.

R3 — Stop treating structural editing instructions as replacement sentences

Observed problem: N1–N3 produce false “not visible” errors despite completed paragraph splits.

Hypothesized cause: clarity_check.py normalizes the paper and searches for the opening or closing words of instructions such as “Split the paragraph.” That cannot verify structural adoption.

Evidence: current parse_notes and main; N1–N3 in the replan’s notes; replan deviation log; reproduced read-only failure. A similar split false positive is recorded in the v14.1 side-study observation.

Proposed change: keep completeness and disposition checks, but do not run prose-substitution matching on structural notes. Either verify explicit paragraph-boundary anchors or classify that part as informational for a narrow manual inspection. Do not add a general semantic-verification agent.

Expected benefit: removes repeated false alarms and temptation to spend a retry merely satisfying a string test.

Potential cost: a small checker branch and fixtures; less automatic assurance for structural edits if using the informational option.

Confidence: high.

Evaluation: rerun the current files and historical split fixtures; retain tests for actual missing prose edits and malformed note schemas. This is distinct from the label-parsing fix already adopted in v14.6.

R4 — Test a smaller editorial pass and a direct strong draft

Observed problem: the clarity pair cost 239,891 tokens while minor scope errors survived; almost every note was applied. The pipeline also hands a detailed strong plan to a cheaper drafter and then back to a strong rewriter.

Hypothesized cause: exhaustive note production, per-note response accounting and repeated whole-paper contexts may have diminishing returns.

Evidence: current E ledger rows; 47 notes, 43 adopted and four adapted; unchanged problematic sentences across final-L8-04.md and final.md; L6/L8 handoff.

Proposed change: trial, separately, a prioritized clarity report without the note-density instruction, and a single constrained editor that edits a disposable copy directly. In a different ablation, replace the cheap draft plus full strong rewrite with one strong draft followed by the existing cold reader and necessary revision. Do not combine ablations initially.

Expected benefit: fewer handoffs and less bookkeeping, with more attention to consequential changes.

Potential cost: source or claim drift; loss of a useful inexpensive draft or fresh editorial perspective.

Confidence: moderate that the overhead is worth testing; low about the saving or quality effect of any specific variant.

Evaluation: use the same frozen pre-edit text and the same research plan for each branch; blind readers judge comprehension, claim preservation and argument quality. Log actual processed usage. Reject the simplification if it saves tokens by erasing qualifications.

R5 — Refresh source obligations when the contribution changes

Observed problem: important antecedents arrived late, and the final check had to repair novelty positioning and attribution after the central contribution had shifted.

Hypothesized cause: acquisition follows the original choice’s list more closely than the evolving argument’s dependencies.

Evidence: Bradley–Saad was already listed as an unavailable lead in the parent candidates; the current cold reader required it and Salib–Goldstein; L8-03 then required explicit credit for belief deletion and engineered consent, and corrected the Long–Sebo–Sims target.

Proposed change: at the existing plan check, when the payoff changes, replace stale source obligations with the nearest argument for that new payoff. Acquire only passages needed for that issue through the existing librarian route. At the next check, ask whether the quoted passage is an endorsement, a report, a concession or a hypothetical. No new standing literature reviewer.

Expected benefit: earlier contextual accuracy and fewer late reframings.

Potential cost: earlier acquisition may prove unnecessary if the new contribution fails.

Confidence: high about late positioning work; moderate about the efficiency benefit.

Evaluation: compare whether new contributions are accurately positioned at plan stage and count later source-driven rewrites. Do not use number of citations as the success metric.

R6 — Make post-send abstract review conditional, and consolidate usage records

These are two small efficiency measures, not new philosophical stages.

Observed problem: the current controller automatically reviews an abstract after a send even though E already actively edited it; usage needed reconstruction from eighteen parent run-log events.

Hypothesized cause: “send” and call completion do not consistently refer to a single verified version and canonical usage record.

Evidence: controller §4g; replan E-01/N4 and E-02/N4; parent calls.csv versus run-log.md. The automatic abstract pass has not yet run for this reviewed paper.

Proposed change: record the hash of the abstract last checked against its body; skip a new abstract-only read when the reviewed version is unchanged, while retaining checks after any actual edit. Expected benefit: avoids potentially several post-send calls for an unchanged abstract and makes efficiency analysis routine.

Potential cost: minor tooling; faulty hash granularity could overlook a relevant body change.

Confidence: high that duplication is possible and usage is fragmented; uncertain about the future abstract-pass cost in this case.

Evaluation: test unchanged text, body-only changes and abstract edits; ensure no semantic change bypasses review. Reconstructed historical totals must match before replacing manual ledger analysis.

8. Proposal A — Repair the existing harness

Keep thesis competition, primary-source obligations, the strong plan checker, cold reader, integrated rewrite, conditional independent final check, quotation verification and rendering. Those functions did real work here.

Implement R2 and R3 as small operational fixes. Trial R1’s revised scope test and R5’s changed-contribution source refresh within existing calls. Test R6’s version-based suppression of redundant abstract work. Retain the present two-call clarity route until R4’s ablation provides evidence for changing it, but remove pressure to manufacture exhaustive low-value notes.

Delete duplication rather than add reviewers: no extra general significance gate, no universal second final check, no model retry for a structural string-match failure, and no abstract review solely because a send flag was set.

Expected gain is modest but credible: fewer wasted calls and more precise existing criticism. The risk is that additional routing exceptions complicate an already long controller. Implement rules in tools where they are mechanical, and replace prose rather than appending another layer.

9. Proposal B — A six-call research and drafting baseline

After a reusable regional corpus exists, try this substantially simpler alternative:

  1. A strong researcher develops three short competing arguments from opened focal passages, identifying nearest antecedents, payoff and a countercase.
  2. A fresh strong adjudicator selects a supported contribution, narrows it, or stops. Retrieval of a decisive missing text is one conditional cheaper call.
  3. One strong author writes the full paper from the selected argument and those sources; no separate 3,000-word outline and cheap prose draft.
  4. A fresh strong cold reader assesses argument, significance, scope and a short list of comprehension failures.
  5. One strong author revises, or stops if the requested repair requires a new research result. A genuinely new central argument gets the existing conditional fresh check.
  6. A cheaper verifier checks references and quotations and renders the result.

Do not count source access or human retrieval as free. The six-call baseline can become seven or eight when justified; its point is to remove routine handoffs, not conceal work behind a call count.

This drops the separate cheap draft, exhaustive editorial notes and response ledger, routine final criticism of unchanged arguments, and automatic abstract reprocessing. It preserves independent selection and criticism and the distinction between research and mere polishing.

Its main risk is cognitive overload in the author call and late discovery of problems a detailed plan would have exposed. That is why it is a competing experimental baseline, not the recommended immediate replacement. The present run’s improvement gives Proposal A a practical advantage; only comparative evidence can establish whether B offers similar or better work for less computation.

10. Experiments and decision points

Start with tests that do not require a new paper.

  • Operational replay: reproduce the malformed setup and structural notes on disposable fixtures. R2/R3 should prevent or classify the failure without calling a model.
  • Scope test: give old and revised critic prompts the same three argument excerpts, including one valid conditional construction and one overbroad prohibition. Assess false accusations as well as missed gaps. Do not make the present objection a grading key every critic is primed to repeat.
  • Editorial ablation: compare the same pre-clarity version under the existing pair, a prioritized pair and a direct editor. Have blind readers explain the thesis and identify conditions before judging style.
  • Drafting ablation: use one saved plan with the current Sonnet-to-Opus route and the direct-strong-author route. Preserve cold criticism in both.
  • Pipeline pilot: only after those small tests, compare A and B on at least three different philosophical problems. Compare intermediate proposals and arguments before funding all the drafts. A weaker project should stop at selection.

At selection, decide whether the supported result is worth writing, rather than whether a sufficiently bold advertised result would be. At a changed hinge, decide whether to acquire one decisive source, narrow, or stop. At a final repair, distinguish a new argument from clarification. After a favorable external review, compare the expected gain of each local fix with its cost; do not automatically launch another cycle.

Use blind comparative placement, calibration, the accuracy of source interpretation, survival of serious countercases, comprehension and total measured usage together. A higher send rate alone could mean a lower standard. A lower token total alone could mean worse research.
