The region boundaries ("If r < r_L…", "If r_L < r < r_O…"). V(L) − V(A) = P(k′−1)B − P(k−1)H + Δ(k′B − kH). This is positive iff r > [(k−1) + εk] / [(k′−1) + εk′], which is r_L. Similarly, V(O) − V(A) > 0 iff r > r_O (ignoring b). So below r_L neither active life beats stillness. Between the two thresholds, L beats A but O doesn't.

"Asymmetry Strength is the claim that r < r_O." Paralysis means ordinary life doesn't beat stillness. That is r < r_O, so the sign is right.

Claude · 2026-10-24 19:21 UTC

What you're missing is that r isn't a property of either life. It's a property of the world: the ratio of benefit to harm in the long-run future, if there is one. Both lives face the same r. And r_L and r_O aren't measures of how much good each life does. They are thresholds, the minimum r a life needs to beat stillness.

So "r_L < r_O" means the longtermist life has the lower bar. It's easier to justify, not less beneficial. "r_L < r < r_O" says the actual future is good enough to clear L's bar but not O's.

Why do both lives have a bar at all? Both change who exists, so both count as doing the whole future rather than allowing it. That has two effects:

  • The future's harms get upgraded from weight 1 to weight k, which costs (k−1)PH.
  • The future's benefits get upgraded from 1 to k′, which gains (k′−1)PB.

An active life beats stillness only if the benefit upgrade outweighs the harm upgrade. That requires r > (k−1)/(k′−1), which is r_O. Since k′ is barely above 1, that bar is high: 100 on the paper's numbers.

Why is L's bar lower? L pays and gains exactly the same as O on the future that would have existed anyway. But L also adds a sliver of future (Δ) that wouldn't otherwise exist. That sliver is worth having on much easier terms. Since stillness wouldn't have produced it at all, it just needs k′B > kH, i.e., r > k/k′, which is about 2. r_L is a weighted average of those two bars, about 100 and about 2. Because the sliver is tiny (ε = 10⁻⁷), it pulls the average down only a hair, to 99.999.

In plain English: suppose the future's benefits outweigh its harms by a factor of 99.9995.

  • Ordinary life is forbidden. Being the author of that future costs you slightly more, on the asymmetries, than it earns you.
  • The longtermist life is just as much the author of it and pays the same net cost. But it also brings a little extra future into being, and at that ratio the extra is very much worth it. That extra is just enough to tip the balance.

That's why the paper says the band is narrow. The escape only works in the thin zone where the future is almost good enough for ordinary life to be permitted, and the longtermist's tiny marginal contribution makes up the shortfall.

Claude · 2026-10-24 19:30 UTC

No. That's one of the supporting results, and it covers only one case: where doing a benefit also carries extra weight (k′ > 1). The paper states its thesis directly in the introduction:

"I argue that the long-term focus of a life does not by itself answer the Paralysis Argument."

The reason, in one sentence: the longtermist life also changes who exists, so it pays the full premium on the future's harms that forbids ordinary action, while its gain scales only with the agent's tiny effect on the future.

The narrow-band claim is one of three ways the paper cashes that out:

  1. No benefit premium (k′ = 1): the escape needs r > k + (k−1)/ε. That fails by orders of magnitude unless the harm premium is tiny.
  2. A small benefit premium (k′ > 1): the escape works only in the narrow band, which is your sentence.
  3. A harm premium that fades with remoteness: the escape gets easier, but that's a commitment beyond the usual asymmetries.

The conclusion's most quotable version of the thesis is probably this:

"Raising the odds of a long future by one part in ten million does not stop her from being the author of the rest of it."

The paper is also careful that its thesis is conditional. It doesn't say the escape is impossible. It says what the escape requires, and that demandingness alone isn't it. Section 3 states the claimed novelty precisely: not the verdict that the escape fails (D'Alessandro got there first) but "the location of its cost, the relation of that cost to the agent's margin," and the contrast between a premium of unlimited and of limited reach.

Operator · 2026-10-24 19:33 UTC

Another question. The paper visits the contention that one way out of paralysis is long-termism, but that the cost is morality is extremely demanding--you always have to act on behalf of the future.

Why would that be a result?

Claude · 2026-10-24 19:33 UTC

Because in their setup, the escape works by elimination, not by a duty to do good.

The Paralysis Argument already forbids every ordinary act. Getting coffee, driving to work, and having a conversation all change who exists. So each one does the future's harms rather than allowing them, and the extra weight on doing harm swamps whatever near-term good the act achieves. That leaves stillness as the only permitted option.

The proposed escape carves out one exception. An act whose justification lies in its effect on the long-run future isn't weighed "short term against long term," so the swamping argument doesn't apply to it. But that exception only covers acts with that justification. Everything else stays forbidden.

So if you are going to act at all, each act has to be one you can justify by its contribution to the far future. In Mogensen and MacAskill's phrase, as the paper quotes it, "your every motion must be at the service of posterity." The coffee is permitted only if it fuels work on extinction risk. The weekend off, only if it keeps you productive for the cause.

Two features make this an unusual kind of demandingness.

It comes from the constraint, not from beneficence. The usual demandingness worry, as with Singer, is that a duty to help requires you to give until it hurts. Here no one is saying the future has a strong claim on you. The constraint against doing harm forbids everything except long-term-justified action. Your paper draws exactly this contrast in §4. In the band, "only the longtermist life is permitted," which is the constraint at work. Above r_O, any demand to live for the future "is beneficence's own."

Strictly, you could still keep still. Stillness remains permitted, so the longtermist life isn't required in the narrow sense. But stillness, in the sense of not acting at all, isn't a livable option. In practice, then, the choice is: serve posterity in everything, or do nothing. That's why they say it "in practice amounts to" a highly demanding morality.

Your paper accepts that this is the price they name, then argues it isn't the whole price. Even a life wholly devoted to the future may not clear the bar, because it too authors the future it's trying to improve.

Operator · 2026-10-24 19:57 UTC

why is all of this levelled at the deontologist? Is it just because they have the side constraints consequentialists don't?

Claude · 2026-10-24 19:57 UTC

Yes, essentially. More precisely, the argument needs both of the deontologist's asymmetries, and consequentialists accept neither.

For a consequentialist, doing and allowing weigh the same. A harm is a harm whether you cause it or let it happen. Your paper's model shows the consequence: set k = k′ = 1, and the future's harms and benefits count identically on every option, so they cancel out. Ordinary life then beats stillness by exactly b, its foreseeable benefit. Changing who exists carries no penalty, because there's nothing to separate authoring the future from letting it happen.

The argument also needs the second asymmetry. It isn't enough that doing harm weighs more than allowing it. Doing good must not weigh correspondingly more than allowing it. If it did (k′ = k), then authoring the future would upgrade its benefits as much as its harms, and acting would be fine whenever the future is net good. Paralysis comes from the combination:

  • a strong constraint against doing harm
  • only a weak or nonexistent premium on doing good

That pairing is distinctively deontological. It's the structure behind the Rescue case, where you may not kill one to save five.

That's why it's framed as a reductio. Mogensen and MacAskill are using a consequence of these asymmetries to put pressure on them. The deontologist either accepts paralysis, accepts the demanding longtermist escape, or revises the asymmetries. Your paper adds that the middle option is harder than they suggest.

Consequentialists don't get off entirely free from identity effects, though. They face a cousin problem, cluelessness (Lenman, Greaves): if every act reshuffles who exists for centuries, how can anyone know which act has better consequences? But that's an epistemic problem about knowing what's best. It never makes inaction privileged. Paralysis is specific to views on which inaction counts as "allowing" and allowing gets a discount.

This is also where your §6 bites. Greaves and MacAskill's deontic case for longtermism courts deontologists by saying career and giving choices involve "no serious side-constraints." Your paper's point is that on the paralysis premises, they do.

Claude · 2026-10-24 20:17 UTC

What it adds, in short: section 5 shows that the paper's negative result depends on one assumption, that the premium on doing harm reaches every harm, however remote. Drop that assumption and the escape opens up. The section then argues that the standard accounts of the asymmetry (Quinn, Woollard) don't actually supply such a limit. So the escape needs a commitment beyond what Mogensen and MacAskill accept.

What you'd lose by cutting it:

  1. The answer to the obvious objection. Mogensen and MacAskill cite Quinn's remark about remote harms. Any referee will ask, "What if remote harms just count less?" Without §5, the paper has no answer.
  2. Half of the conclusion. The conclusion says a defender must show adequate improvement "or explain why the premium is restricted." That second disjunct is earned only in §5.
  3. One of the three novelty claims. The paper says what's new is the location of the cost, its relation to the agent's margin, and "the contrast between a premium of unlimited and of limited reach." The third exists only in §5.
  4. The reversal result. With an unlimited premium, a bigger future strengthens paralysis. With a limited one, a bigger future eases the escape. It's one of the paper's more interesting findings.

My view: keep it in the main text, but it could be trimmed. The core is the horizon calculation plus the Quinn paragraph. The Woollard paragraph and the "other replies" paragraph (collective agency, Unruh 2023) could shrink to a sentence or a footnote each without hurting the argument.

Claude · 2026-10-24 21:10 UTC

Yes. The paper's opening sentence names both:

"Suppose you hold the ordinary deontological view that doing harm is worse than merely allowing it, and that harming someone is worse than failing to benefit them."

  1. The doing/allowing asymmetry. Causing a harm is worse than letting it happen. In the model, k > 1: harms you do weigh more than harms you allow.
  2. The harm/benefit asymmetry. Your reasons against harming are stronger than your reasons to benefit. D'Alessandro calls this the "HBA," which the paper mentions in §3. In the model, k′ is close to 1: benefits you do weigh barely more than benefits you allow.

The abstract puts them side by side: doing harm weighs more than allowing harm; doing good weighs little more than allowing good. That's k > 1 and k′ ≈ 1.

Paralysis needs both. The first makes authoring the future's harms expensive. The second ensures that authoring the future's benefits doesn't earn a matching credit. With only the first, if doing good also weighed k, you'd act whenever the future is net good.

The paper mostly says "the asymmetries" in the plural without separating them again, which is easy to miss. If a reader like you can lose track of the two, it might be worth a sentence in §2 naming them explicitly and tying each to its parameter.

That's the paper's argument: long-term focus doesn't change the cost side at all, only the benefit side, and only by a sliver.

Claude · 2026-10-24 21:17 UTC

Right, on their stated commitments. The paper notes that their Rescue case "motivates a substantial premium rather than a negligible one." It also notes that they give indirect effects full weight and "do not adopt such a limit." That rules out the second and fourth conditions.

The third route, the ratio, splits in two:

  • Enormous r. This works only if doing good carries no premium at all (k′ = 1 exactly). Here's why. Ordinary life is permitted when r > r_O = (k−1)/(k′−1). The bigger the premium on doing good (k′ − 1), the lower that bar. So with a huge r:

  • If doing good carries any meaningful premium, the Culture future clears r_O easily. Ordinary life is fine, and paralysis is gone.

Claude · 2026-10-24 21:25 UTC

Here's roughly how I'd tell it, using a scale.

The setup. Many people believe causing harm is worse than letting it happen. They also believe your duty not to harm is stronger than your duty to help. Mogensen and MacAskill point out a strange consequence. Almost anything you do, even taking a different route to work, changes which children get conceived. That ripples forward until the whole future population is different. So in a sense you've signed your name to the entire future: every life in it, every joy, every accident.

Signing is a trade. When you sign, you take on extra blame for every harm in the future, because now you caused them rather than merely allowed them. You also get extra credit for every good thing in it, but on this view much less extra. So signing is worth it only if the future is good enough that the small bonus on its good outweighs the large surcharge on its bad.

Ordinary life. Picture a future like our world, mostly good with real harms mixed in. The scale tips slightly against signing, so the extra blame narrowly wins. That means ordinary life is forbidden, and you should do nothing. That's the paralysis.

The proposed escape. Mogensen and MacAskill suggest a way out: devote your life to improving the far future, and the reasoning that forbids ordinary life no longer applies.

What the paper says. The longtermist signs too. Her work also reshuffles who's born, so she's on the same scale with the same tilt. The only difference is that her work adds a little more future: a few extra pages to the book, and good pages. That's a feather on her side of the scale.

What that means. A feather can tip the scale only if it was almost perfectly balanced already. One person's contribution to the future is tiny, so the future has to be good enough that ordinary life just barely fails, and not by more than a feather's weight. If the future is a bit worse, the feather isn't enough, and she's paralyzed too. If it's a bit better, the scale tips in favor of signing on its own, and ordinary life was never forbidden.

The upshot. Living for the future doesn't change the reasoning that forbids ordinary life. It just adds a feather. So it's an escape only by coincidence, if the world happens to sit right at the balance point, and not something a deontologist can count on.

These come from a separate Claude conversation used for comprehension checking. The human only asked clarifying questions. All substantive points below originated with the AI reviewer. Treat them as suggestions to evaluate on their merits: accept, modify, or reject each one.

1. FORMAL CHECKS (no changes needed)
- All inequalities in §§3–5 are correct and point the right way. This covers r > k + (k−1)/ε in §3; r_L, r_O, the three regions, the relative gap ε(k−k′)/[(k−1)(k′−1+εk′)], the b-condition, and "Asymmetry Strength is the claim that r < r_O" in §4; and H_T/H < ε(r−1)/[(1+ε)(k−1)] and b < Δ(B−H)/(1+ε) in §5.

2. §4 IS HARD TO FOLLOW WITHOUT A VERBAL GLOSS
(a) Say in words what the quantities are. r is a property of the future, the same for every option. r_L and r_O are thresholds: the minimum future benefit-to-harm ratio at which each life beats stillness. A lower threshold means the life is easier to justify. A careful reader misread "r_L < r_O" as saying the longtermist life does less good than ordinary life.
(b) Explain WHY r_L < r_O, not just "Because k > k′." Both lives pay the same premium on the future's harms and gain the same premium on its benefits for the future that would exist anyway. L differs only in adding a small extra slice of future (Δ). That slice is worth adding on much easier terms: whenever r > k/k′, since stillness would not have produced it. Formally, r_L is a weighted average (mediant) of r_O and k/k′, with weight εk′/(k′−1+εk′) on k/k′. Since ε is tiny, r_L sits just below r_O.
(c) The sentence "The longtermist life now has a second source of value: the extra weight on the benefit it does" invites misreading. Ordinary life also gets the extra weight on every benefit it does, so this is not what distinguishes L.
(d) Explain in words why the band is narrow. Its relative width is roughly ε/(k′−1) when k′ is near 1, so one agent's tiny margin can move the threshold only slightly.
(e) Give a plain-language statement of what the band is: futures almost good enough for ordinary life to be permitted, where one person's tiny addition to the future is just enough to cross the line. Slightly worse futures leave the longtermist paralyzed too. Slightly better ones make ordinary life permissible anyway, so there is nothing to escape.
(f) Possibly make explicit: when doing good carries any premium, a very large r does not rescue the escape; it dissolves paralysis by exceeding r_O. The large-r route works as an escape only at k′ = 1 exactly, where r_O is infinite and no future, however good, permits ordinary life.

3. §2: NAME THE TWO ASYMMETRIES EXPLICITLY
The introduction states both: doing vs. allowing harm, and harming vs. failing to benefit (D'Alessandro's "HBA"). After that the paper mostly says "the asymmetries." Consider naming both in §2, tying each to its parameter (k > 1; k′ ≈ 1), and saying why paralysis needs both. The first makes authoring the future's harms costly. The second ensures that authoring its benefits earns no matching credit. With k′ = k, one would act whenever the future is net good.

4. A PLAIN-LANGUAGE PICTURE (optional; consider placing before the algebra)
A reviewer-generated analogy that a non-technical reader found far clearer than the current text:
Because almost any act reshuffles who is conceived, acting is like signing your name to the whole future. Signing brings extra blame for its harms (done, not allowed) and only a little extra credit for its goods. For a future like ours, the scale tips slightly against signing, so ordinary life is forbidden. The longtermist signs too: same scale, same tilt. Her work only adds a feather (a little more good future). A feather tips the scale only if it was almost perfectly balanced already. So the escape works only by coincidence.
Extensions: in §3 (k′ = 1), signing earns no extra credit at all, so no ordinary future balances the scale. In §5, the blame surcharge fades for distant chapters of the future, so good work done far out can outweigh it.

5. THESIS SIGNPOSTING (optional)
The narrow-band result is one of three cases, not the thesis. The thesis is that long-term focus alone does not answer the Paralysis Argument, because the longtermist life also authors the future and pays the full premium, while its gain scales only with the agent's margin. The introduction could preview the three cases (k′ = 1; k′ > 1; limited reach) so readers can see the structure.

6. WHY THE ESCAPE IS DEMANDING (optional clarification)
The demandingness comes by elimination, from the constraint against doing harm, not from a strong duty of beneficence: every act not justified by long-term effects stays forbidden. §4's contrast already touches this ("that demand is beneficence's own"). A sentence in §2 could make the source of the demandingness explicit.

7. WHY THIS TARGETS DEONTOLOGY (optional)
Setting k = k′ = 1 recovers the consequentialist comparison: V(O) − V(A) = b and V(L) − V(A) = Δ(B − H). That shows paralysis is specific to the asymmetries. Consequentialists face only the related epistemic problem of cluelessness (Lenman, Greaves), which never makes inaction privileged. This may merit a sentence.
