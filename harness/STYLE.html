What the paper must be (v12; clarity v14.1): for the planner, drafter, rewriter and editor

The standard. A question the reader already cares about; one clear central claim that someone in print would have to give up; a spine the reader can follow; only the objections that spine needs; enough orientation to see why it matters. If the thesis, granted, would change nothing a specialist believes or does, it is not yet a paper.

The opening. Pose the question before the thesis. State the thesis plainly by the end of the introduction. Say what is at stake and whose view it is. Enter the debate by name. A short roadmap, or none.

Structure follows the argument. Each section does one step the argument needs, in the order a reader needs it. No section exists to defend the thesis's status or to classify positions. Answer objections where they arise; the strongest objection gets a section only if answering it advances the claim. The introduction is about 600 to 800 words and the conclusion 300 to 400.

Engagement. Name the authors you answer and quote them in their own words, with page. Say plainly where you agree with them.

Prose. Write like a good journal article: varied sentences, concrete examples, claims made and then supported. No throat-clearing, no announcing what you are about to do in every paragraph, no restating what was just said, no lists in the body, no stock phrases ("it is important to note", "crucially", "in this paper I will"). Footnotes are few and short.

Clarity (v14.1). Write for a smart philosopher outside the subfield. Introduce the view under discussion, and what makes it attractive and vulnerable, before surveying replies to it. Give the reader the shape of an argument in plain words before its exact formulation. Put your own explanatory sentence first and the quotation that supports it second; quote where the wording matters, paraphrase where it only records a position. One argumentative job per paragraph. Announce an objection as an objection before voicing it. At the major transitions, state the claim in full rather than a shorthand verdict. Each return to the thesis does a distinct job. Habits that belong in the genre stay: numbered premises, "one might object", stipulations, calibrated hedges, restating the thesis at beginnings and endings. To save words, delete; never merge sentences or pack clauses. The title says what the paper is about and is no larger than the result; section titles say what each section does.

The abstract (v14.4). It is written for a philosopher outside the subfield, in at most 150 words, and it follows the journal convention in this order: the question or problem, and whose view is the target (one or two sentences, with any term of art the reader needs explained in plain words or avoided); the thesis, in one plain sentence beginning "I argue that" or the like; the main steps of the argument, in the order the paper takes them, one short sentence each; the upshot or what is at stake. It names the target author and at most one other. It has no quotations, citations or page references, no shorthand verdicts the reader must translate, no coined labels the body has not yet introduced, and no sentence over about 30 words or with a stack of subordinate clauses. It claims no more than the body shows, and a conditional result is stated as conditional. The abstract is not a compressed copy of the introduction: if the body takes ten sentences to build an inference, the abstract gives it the plain gist in one or two and leaves the exact formulation to the body.

Length. Everything counts: title, abstract, body, notes and references. Target about 5,300 words; never over 5,500. References in author-date style, as # References {-}; sections as # Title (no number: the template numbers them); notes as [^n].
