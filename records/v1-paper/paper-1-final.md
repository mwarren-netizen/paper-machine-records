# Counted, Not Caused: Self-Justification at the Enkratic Stop

## 1. The stop and its charge

Tarsney's enkratic externalism is built to end a regress.[^1] The regress is a conditional argument whose conclusion is that internalism is false (ms. pp. 9–10). Tarsney runs it first for unbounded agents and then carries it to bounded ones (ms. pp. 8, 11). Internalists have escapes, and convergence is one (ms. pp. 13–14). Tarsney also gives a dominance case against first-order externalism (ms. pp. 23–24). I take no side on the regress, its premises, or these escapes. My question concerns only where the regress is made to stop.

It stops at a single comprehensive enkratic norm with belief-independent force (ms. p. 16). There, "the regress of higher-order norms simply stops at the second order" (ms. p. 17). Rationality, on this view, is responding appropriately to one's beliefs about objective reasons (ms. p. 17). The stop, as I reconstruct it, is therefore one comprehensive, second-order enkratic norm with belief-independent force, which weighs the agent's credences about objective reasons. Its "plain-vanilla example" is MEC, the norm to maximize expected choiceworthiness (ms. p. 20). The formulation Tarsney himself favours is SD, a stochastic-dominance norm (ms. p. 20 n. 22).

Tarsney leaves open RR, the thesis that an option's being rationally required gives an objective reason for it: rationality is conceptually distinct from objective reasons, but "It does not, in particular, require us to deny ... that facts about rationality constitute objective reasons" (ms. p. 18). A reflexive reason favours an option for its standing under the weighing norm, as the agent takes it or as it is. In a near-tie, the first-order margin is below the reflexive reason's weight, discounted by the agent's credence in RR. My anchors are the independence conclusion, "... but not on your beliefs about rationality" (ms. p. 25), and n. 25's charge of self-justification against internalist restrictions.

My thesis is that Tarsney's stop readmits the self-justification he charges against internalism. Because the stop weighs the agent's credences about objective reasons, an agent with any credence in RR can, in any near-tie, make an option rationally required under MEC by believing it required. Weighing no reflexive reason, a repair I call Exclusion, mends the stop without arbitrariness or loss of guidance. Section 2 establishes the dependence, and §3 its conflict with ms. p. 25. §§4–6 then take, one each, the three branches of the dilemma this forces on the stop's defender. I do not read the formal fallibility argument (ms. pp. 21–22) as entailing Exclusion.

## 2. The near-tie

The model is small. There are two options, A and B, and the first-order facts, of which the agent is certain, favour A by d > 0. She has credence r > 0 in RR, and credences qA and qB that A, or B, is rationally required. Where RR holds, a required option has a reflexive reason of weight w on MEC's shared cardinal scale (ms. p. 17 n. 19). With B's first-order choiceworthiness at 0, three hypotheses matter:

- no reflexive reason (RR fails, or neither option is required): A scores d, B 0;
- RR, and A required (credence r·qA): A scores d + w, B 0;
- RR, and B required (credence r·qB): A scores d, B w.

Hence EC(B) − EC(A) = w·r·(qB − qA) − d.

The credences r·qA and r·qB assume that her credence in RR does not shift with her view of which option is required. Her conditional credences in RR are held fixed, at r. The reflexive reason is also additive, a single weight added to the required option. Both are inputs of the model.

Nor does r > 0 depart from the target. Tarsney's n. 27 (ms. p. 24) contemplates an agent who believes she has "only pro tanto reason, to be rational". Such an agent is inside his own view.

From the formula, MEC requires B just when qB − qA > d/(w·r). Suppose a near-tie: the margin condition holds, w·r > d; the threshold is then below 1. Two coherent states are open to her. In one she is confident enough that B is required for qB − qA to exceed d/(w·r); MEC then requires B. In the other she is confident that A is required; qB − qA is negative, and MEC requires A. The states differ only in her beliefs about which option is required; her first-order credences and her view of RR are the same in both. So in any choice with w·r > d, MEC's verdict follows her belief about which option is required.

For instance, let d = 1, w = 4 and r = 1/2, so w·r = 2 > 1 and the threshold is 1/2. If qB = 0.9 and qA = 0.1, then EC(B) − EC(A) = 2 × 0.8 − 1 = 0.6, and B is required. If instead qA = 0.9 and qB = 0.1, it is 2 × (−0.8) − 1 = −2.6, and A is required.

The dependence does not rest on her being mistaken. Suppose she is unbounded and credits MEC to degree m, with w·r·m > d, and that her credence in rival norms favours neither option's being required. Then in each state she is right about what MEC selects there. That is why n. 27's remark on "perfect introspective access" does not remove the dependence: introspective certainty about what MEC selects is correct in either state.

MEC is an illustration. SD is the formulation Tarsney himself favours (ms. p. 20 n. 22), so a result on SD is not confined to one. Note 22 defines SD thus: "O is rationally prohibited iff there is another option P such that (i) for any degree of choiceworthiness, P is at least as likely as O to be at least that choiceworthy and (ii) for some degree of choiceworthiness, P is strictly more likely to be at least that choiceworthy". Read "permitted" as "undominated", and let w > d.

On the model, A's choiceworthiness is d + w with credence r·qA, and d otherwise; B's is w with credence r·qB, and 0 otherwise. A reaches every level up to d for certain. Between d and w, A reaches a level with credence r·qA and B with r·qB; above w, only A can. So A stochastically dominates B just when qA ≥ qB. And B never dominates A while r·qB < 1, since B may then fall short of d.

Hence her belief that A is required makes A required, and her belief that B is required makes A unrequired, not B required. The restriction is part of the result: on SD, the dependence concerns only A's requirement and B's permission. The SD result assumes no background uncertainty, since she is certain of the first-order facts; I do not show that it survives such uncertainty.

The dependence can now be stated whole, scope included. In a choice between two options, with one additive reflexive reason of a single weight, MEC's verdict follows the agent's belief about which option is required whenever w·r > d, her first-order credences and her view of RR held fixed. On SD, whenever w > d, her belief settles A's requirement and B's permission. Not every agent faces a near-tie. But the result holds for every choice that meets the margin condition.

The model has one input a defender can contest: additivity. To contest it, though, is to contest the model's inputs, not the result given those inputs. This defends the result, not the input. Note 19 puts reflexive reasons on the shared scale without making them additive; and if the RR she credits adds no reason beyond those that make an option required, or only breaks exact ties, there is no near-tie while d > 0. I do not show that the RR Tarsney leaves open is additive.

Two neighbours help to place the result. Weatherson's same-evaluation principles make normative uncertainty incoherent (2019, site p. 40). The loop found here does not: it yields several coherent states, each self-confirming. And the dependence runs through her credence in RR, not through RR's truth, so Broome's scepticism about RR does not touch it.

## 3. What the dependence contradicts

One way to misread the dependence is as the regress returning. It is not that. In Tarsney's terms, what §2 found is sensitivity (ms. p. 4), not restriction (ms. p. 6): what MEC requires varies with her beliefs about rationality, as, on SD, does whether A is required.

A second misreading would have me claim that MEC lacks second-order standing, in the sense in which Tarsney orders norms (ms. pp. 5–6). I make no such claim. In particular, I do not argue that a defect in assigning MEC its order re-establishes a regress of domain restrictions. I also leave open whether the dependence bears on the claim that the regress stops at the second order (ms. p. 17).

Three passages bear on independence, and the dependence meets each differently.

The broad one is ms. p. 25: what one rationally ought to do does not depend on one's beliefs about rationality. For the agent of §2 it fails. Leave her view of RR alone, and every coherent variation from confidence that A is required to confidence that B is required changes MEC's verdict, and on SD, Tarsney's preferred formulation, whether A is required. That is sensitivity to beliefs about rationality, which ms. p. 25 rules out.

Note 18 (ms. p. 16) reads: "Thus, varying an agent's beliefs about objective norms in isolation (holding all other features of a choice situation fixed) can change the expected choiceworthiness of her options, but varying her beliefs about subjective norms cannot". It can be read two ways, and the text leaves both open. Hold fixed her attitudes other than her beliefs about which option is required, and n. 18 is false for her, since those beliefs alone move the verdict. Hold fixed every objective-reason credence, and n. 18 is not contradicted; but, so read, it cannot certify MEC's second-order standing for her, though I do not argue this further. That is no claim that MEC lacks such standing.

The qualified one is ms. p. 18, which as printed says "... does not make them (directly) sensitive to your beliefs about rationality". That claim survives. I grant it.

Could the dependence be consistent with independence after all? A defender could say so on Tarsney's behalf; I treat what follows as a charitable extension of the target, not a claim Tarsney makes. Its textual basis is the parenthesis in ms. p. 18, "(directly)", and n. 27's remark that a belief about irrationality "is not in itself a belief about objective reasons" (ms. p. 24). The thought is that her beliefs about rationality move MEC's verdict only through an objective-reason belief, her credence in RR. So the dependence is mediated; and mediated dependence, the defender says, is consistent with independence.

The difficulty is that RR is option-neutral. It favours neither A nor B: it says only that whichever is required has a reason for it. Look at what the weighing sees: EC(B) − EC(A) = w·r·(qB − qA) − d. Her credence in RR enters only as the multiplier r. The inputs that discriminate between the options are d, which favours A, and qB − qA, her credences about the weighing norm's own verdict. Mediation adds no option-discriminating input.

The defender, then, can keep narrower claims: the qualified claim of ms. p. 18, or n. 18 read as holding every objective-reason credence fixed. What the defender cannot keep with them is ms. p. 25. What she rationally ought to do still turns on her beliefs about rationality, and the case that the dependence contradicts the independence conclusion is complete.

That holds on the broad reading of ms. p. 25 taken here; I do not exclude a narrower one. A defender may count r·qB, her credence that B has a reflexive reason, as the option-discriminating input, since it is a credence about objective reasons; and n. 27 (ms. p. 24), in allowing that beliefs about rationality may come with objective-reason beliefs that count only pro tanto, may foresee the channel. Read so, ms. p. 25 survives, with no more content than n. 18 on its second reading.

Tarsney leaves RR open, and the dependence does not wait on RR's truth. His stop must therefore do one of three things with reflexive reasons. It may count them as she takes them; that is MEC as stated. It may count them at their actual standing; that needs a construction of mine, MEC\*, given in §5. Or it may weigh none; that is Exclusion, a restriction of his enkratic conception. There is no fourth branch: a reflexive reason that is weighed is keyed to the option's standing either as she takes it or as it is. I attribute to Tarsney neither a denial of RR nor Exclusion.

## 4. First branch: counting reflexive reasons as she takes them

Fallibility does not decide this branch. Tarsney's Argument from Fallibility (ms. pp. 21–22) demands that false beliefs about what rationality requires be possible. Against MEC the demand meets a ready answer, the limited-fallibility defence. Its basis is Tarsney's own sentence: enkratic externalism is "entirely compatible with agents having false beliefs about rationality" (ms. p. 22). Applying it to MEC against this demand is my extension, not his claim. MEC does leave confident error possible, and the defence should be granted its point. Yet possible error is something a norm allows somewhere. It does not tell us whether the norm is self-justifying where it counts the belief. The question is self-justification, not fallibility.

Two notes on the argument's setting. Tarsney ties it to Weatherson: n. 24 cites Weatherson (2014, p. 146) on ProbWrong, and ms. p. 10 n. 11 relates Weatherson's argument to the Argument from Fallibility. And, as §1 announced, I do not read the formal argument as entailing Exclusion.

If the question is self-justification, the text to consult is n. 25 (ms. p. 22). This section turns on its wording, so it is given whole:

> The same worry arises, to only a slightly lesser degree, if the threshold t is lower. At the extreme, suppose the internalist only asserts that an agent cannot be rationally permitted to choose an option that she is certain is rationally impermissible, or prohibited from choosing an option that she is certain is permissible. Then certainty in rational permissions or prohibitions is self-fulfilling and self-justifying. If I become certain that an option O is rationally permissible (or impermissible), no matter how irrationally, my belief is guaranteed to be correct, and I am justified in maintaining it in the face of any new evidence or arguments.

The wording fixes three things. The charge is not all-or-nothing: as the threshold falls, it applies "to only a slightly lesser degree". Even the extreme case guarantees correctness only to certainty. And "self-justifying" has a stated content, which the last clause gives: justified persistence against "any new evidence or arguments".

The note therefore condemns even a weak internalist restriction, one that guarantees correctness only above a threshold. A defender who would acquit MEC and keep that charge needs a principled line, other than size or route, between the restriction's class and MEC's near-tie class; and the line must leave the charge standing.

The note opens by extending a worry stated just before it (ms. p. 22): that beliefs about rational requirement, if they fixed what rationality requires, would seem to have no truth conditions but themselves. I read the charge through the note's gloss instead. I do not take up that worry, or the note's role in carrying the Argument from Fallibility to low thresholds, and the reading of its last clause used below is not defended against them.

In n. 25's terms, the near-tie looks like this. In §2's instance, both of her confident states are coherent, and in each her belief is true. It is self-confirming: true partly because held, and not through anything it causes. The norm counts the belief, through RR, for the option it names; RR by itself favours neither option. With r, w and d fixed, the norm grades no confident answer wrong.

In general MEC requires B just when qB − qA > d/(w·r); here, when the difference exceeds one half. Above that confidence margin her belief that B is required is guaranteed correct, and her belief that A is required is correct whenever qA ≥ qB. That is the shape of the restriction n. 25 condemns, reached by weighing.

Two candidate lines can be tested quickly. The first is size. However small MEC's near-tie class may be, its size settles how many choices are affected. It does not settle whether, in the choices affected, the belief fixes the verdict it is about. And n. 25 itself keeps the charge as the class shrinks: it presses the charge against a restriction that guarantees correctness only above a threshold.

The second is route. The restriction delivers its guarantee by restricting; MEC reaches the same shape by weighing. But restriction and weighing alike let the belief's content settle the verdict it is about. That the near-tie reaches the restriction's shape by weighing, as shown above, is a difference in how that shape is reached, and so a difference of route only.

Neither size nor route, then, separates the classes.

Immunity to counterevidence is the defender's first resort, because n. 25's own gloss supplies it: a self-justifying belief persists, justified, whatever new evidence or arguments arrive. The proposal is that the restriction's beliefs are immune in this way and the near-tie beliefs are not. On either reading of immunity, the proposal fails.

On the reading the gloss uses, immunity is justified persistence against new evidence about the verdict, for as long as the belief is held above the threshold. The near-tie belief has it. Evidence that bears only on which option is required cannot make the belief false while r, w and d stay fixed: held above the threshold, it is guaranteed correct.

On any stricter reading, the lower-threshold restriction lacks immunity too, since its guarantee also holds only while the belief is held above its threshold. Then n. 25's charge against that restriction lapses.

One difference remains. Evidence about d, r or w can take a choice out of the near-tie class. But that concerns which choices are in the class, a matter of size, not whether the belief is immune within it. Immunity, then, does not separate the classes without costing n. 25 its charge.

Last, a defender might draw the line at mediation. In the near-tie, the belief is self-confirming only through RR, a bridge she accepts; the restriction's self-confirmation runs through no such bridge. Does the bridge matter?

Not to what settles the verdict. RR favours neither option, so it is not an option-discriminating input. With RR in place, the one input that discriminates between A and B, beyond d, is her belief about the norm's own verdict. The self-confirmation described above survives the bridge intact.

So the defender faces a choice. If the line tracks what settles the verdict, it finds her belief doing so on both sides, and the classes stay unseparated. If it tracks the bridge whatever the bridge contributes, it acquits a belief with the shape n. 25 condemns. The charge would then attach not to that shape but to the want of a credited premise, and n. 25's charge of self-justification would be lost.

The conclusion follows. Size and route do not separate the classes; immunity to counterevidence and mediation by an accepted bridge do not separate them without costing n. 25 its charge. So no principled line other than size or route separates MEC's near-tie class from the weak internalist restriction n. 25 condemns without that cost. The near-tie self-confirmation is the self-justification n. 25 condemns, and n. 25's word "self-justifying" marks the line drawn here, between counting and causing. On this claim the paper's case rests.

It rests there with a limit. The replies on route and mediation take what n. 25 condemns to be a belief's content settling the verdict it is about, and the reply on immunity counts dependence on d, r and w as size. A defender may instead separate a guarantee built into a norm's form, for any agent and content, from one that holds only given her credence in RR and the values of d and w, all revisable by evidence and argument. I do not show that this line reduces to size or route, or costs n. 25 its charge, nor that the four lines tested are the only candidates.

It may be objected that all this concerns a small class of choices. The failure is confined to near-ties; elsewhere MEC is untouched; so MEC remains a standard almost everywhere. Why should a local self-confirmation unseat it?

Because of what the self-confirmation does within its class. A norm that counts a belief about its own verdict for the option the belief names makes contrary beliefs about that verdict each true partly because held. For those beliefs the norm is no standard: with r, w and d fixed, it grades no confident answer wrong. The failure is local in reach, but within its class it is complete. A standard that fails only in some choices still fails, entirely, in those.

The objection also proves too much for the defender. Note 25's charge is local in the same way, since it concerns only beliefs held above a threshold. Whoever dismisses the near-tie as local must dismiss n. 25's charge too.

I do not claim that MEC is no standard outside the class.

Exclusion's first limb can now be stated. The stopping norm aggregates no reason that favours an option for its standing under that norm as she takes it. Beliefs about rationality are not thereby silenced: they count only through the non-reflexive facts they cause or ground.

The ground is the one just given: a norm that counts a belief about its own verdict for the option the belief names is no standard for those beliefs. The ground is not that reflexive reasons are false, nor is it Tarsney's formal fallibility premise, which does not decide the case. Where I call the repaired norm fallible, I mean only this: no belief about the norm's verdicts is made true by the norm's counting it.

Mixed cases call for the counting/causing line. A belief about rationality may be counted and may also cause non-reflexive facts. Suppose her belief that B is required would make doing A distressing. The distress is a non-reflexive fact, and the norm weighs it. What the norm does not weigh is the same belief counted, through RR, for B. The causal channel is weighed; the counting channel is not. Two limits remain. I do not settle whether reasons that a belief about the verdict causes can reopen the loop. And a consideration that favours an option both for its standing and for something else must be split, perhaps indeterminately, by a procedure I do not give.

Exclusion's first limb has neighbours, and each difference should go no further than the texts support. Sepielli remarks on bootstrapping (2014, p. 528), and his "good try" condition does not depend on "whether, as I see things, that action reflects my own beliefs" (p. 529). Kolodny offers bootstrapping as a ground for denying that there are reasons to comply with rational requirements. Exclusion differs from both: it keeps bootstrapped reasons out of one norm's weighing without denying that they exist. On strict liability for norms of rationality, see MacAskill, Bykvist and Ord (2020, p. 108; pp. 31–32; p. 32 n. 34). Exclusion closes a channel they do not consider: the channel through choiceworthiness, by which a belief about the verdict is counted for the option it names. Titelbaum, finally, fits belief to requirement. The norm condemned above does the reverse: by counting her belief, it fits requirement to belief.

## 5. Second branch: counting them at actual standing

The second branch counts reflexive reasons at their actual standing. Call the norm that does so MEC\*. It counts a reflexive reason as it is, not as she takes it: where RR holds, at its full weight w, undiscounted by her credences in RR or about the verdict, for the standing the option in fact has under the stopping norm. So its fixed points need w > d, where MEC's dependence needs w·r > d. MEC\* is the paper's construction, not the target's. It gives the second branch a definite form.

Take a near-tie in which w > d. Call a verdict self-consistent when, with the reflexive reason counted at the standing that verdict assigns, the weighing returns that verdict. There are two.

σA: A is required. The reflexive reason then favours A, adding w to A's first-order lead of d. A leads by d + w and is required, as supposed.

σB: B is required. The reflexive reason then favours B by w, against A's first-order lead of d. B leads by w − d, and since w > d, B is required, as supposed.

"Both permitted" is not self-consistent. If neither option is required, there is no requirement for a reflexive reason to favour, so none is counted. A then leads by d and is required, contrary to the supposition.

Nor does the need for a verdict choose between σA and σB. A stopping norm must return some verdict, and that demand excludes the empty selection, on which it returns neither. It excludes nothing else: σA and σB each meet it.

These fixed points are not Trammell's. His are points of convergence among rival higher-order norms; σA and σB arise within one norm.

Under MEC\*, something must select between σA and σB. Call whatever settles which of them is the norm's verdict a selector. Four candidates can be named, and the verdict of each can be read off the construction.

First, the non-reflexive considerations. The first-order margin favours A by d in σA and σB alike. A selector keyed to it returns σA, which is Exclusion's verdict.

Second, the reflexive considerations, counted at actual standing. These are symmetric. In σA the reflexive reason favours A; in σB it favours B. Each favours whichever verdict is selected, so they give no ground for selecting either. They select nothing.

Third, her beliefs about which option is required. These would select: σA if she believes A required, σB if she believes B required. But a selector keyed to them lets her belief about the verdict fix the verdict. It inherits the defect of the first branch. This is the dependence §4 found to be the self-justification n. 25 condemns, and on §4's ground a norm that settles its verdict by the option her belief about that verdict names is no standard for that belief.

Fourth, nothing. The norm then returns σA or σB for no reason, and the selection is arbitrary.

Whether these four are all the selectors there are needs argument, not assumption. The argument starts from a classification of what a selector could respond to.

Whatever a selector responds to is either a reason or not. If it is a reason, it is non-reflexive or reflexive. The non-reflexive reasons are the first-order considerations, whose balance favours A by d under either verdict; responding to them returns σA. The reflexive reasons, counted at actual standing, favour whichever verdict is selected, and so select nothing.

If it is not a reason, it either favours one option over the other or favours neither. RR is not an option-discriminating input, so the only such input besides d is her belief about the norm's own verdict (§3). A non-reason that favours either option is therefore that belief: the input the enkratic conception makes relevant, and the one whose use as a selector inherits the first branch's defect. A non-reason that favours neither leaves the selection arbitrary.

Hence every selector other than the non-reflexive balance responds to her belief about the verdict, or to no reason, or against the only consideration that distinguishes σA from σB. That consideration is d. The reflexive reason appears in both verdicts, each time on the side of the option required, so it does not distinguish them; d favours A in both. A selector that returns σB goes against d.

The second branch is pressed at two points: whether σA and σB are the only self-consistent verdicts, and whether some selector escapes the four. The first was settled in constructing MEC\*: "both permitted" is not self-consistent, and the need for a verdict excludes only the empty selection. The second is settled by the classification, which leaves a selector nothing further to respond to.

Counted at actual standing, then, the reflexive reason leaves two self-consistent verdicts, σA and σB, and the only selector between them that responds neither to her belief about the verdict, nor to no reason, nor against d is the non-reflexive balance. It returns Exclusion's verdict. So Exclusion withholds from a reflexive reason no verdict it could non-arbitrarily support.

Nor does doing what Exclusion requires set the agent against such a reason. Counted at actual standing, the reflexive reason favours whatever the stopping norm requires. An agent who does what the norm requires never acts against it.

The result is stated for two options and one reflexive reason.

## 6. Third branch: Exclusion and its price

Exclusion can now be stated whole: the stopping norm weighs no reflexive reason, whether counted as she takes it or at actual standing. Its first limb was earned in §4 and its second in §5. Together they are the defender's third branch.

The repair keeps what the target says stops the regress. Exclusion restricts which considerations the norm weighs. It does not restrict which choices the norm governs, and it does not change where the norm's force comes from. So the stop remains what §1 reconstructed: one comprehensive enkratic norm with belief-independent force (ms. pp. 16–17). This is stated here, not taken on as a further burden.

As in §3, I do not claim that the repaired norm has a finite order in Tarsney's sense (ms. pp. 5–6).

Next, guidance. Exclusion is a norm she may doubt. She may give most of her credence to MEC, or to another policy, and little to Exclusion. The question is whether Exclusion can then guide her. It can, in the same way as any doubted stopping norm, the target's included.

The argument has three steps.

(i) Basic guidance under uncertainty is responsiveness to first-order credences through a weighing disposition, whether or not she endorses that disposition.

(ii) Where the policies she credits diverge, a doubted norm is consulted only through acceptance: aiming to choose as the norm recommends (Ross, quoted in MacAskill, Bykvist and Ord 2020, p. 47). Her credences do not settle which norm she consults; the acceptance is one they leave open.

(iii) The stopping norm guides through either route, though her credences do not select it. It can guide her through a weighing disposition, endorsed or not, and it can guide her through her acceptance.

Step (ii) carries the parity, and I do not defend it beyond the sources below.

Together the steps give the conclusion. Any doubted stopping norm, the target's included, guides an agent whose credited policies diverge only through a weighing disposition or an acceptance her credences leave open. This holds of MEC for an agent who doubts it, as much as of Exclusion for an agent who doubts that. There is no third route by which a rival stopping norm could guide her where Exclusion cannot.

So Exclusion costs no guidance its rivals keep. The claim concerns guidance alone; whether Exclusion has other costs is a separate question, taken up below.

The clause "the target's included" has support in the target and in its neighbours, provided their limits are kept.

Tarsney's n. 23 (ms. pp. 20–21) quotes Weatherson's charge (2014, pp. 156–7) that theorising imposes "an external standard on an agent, one that an agent may, in good faith and something like good conscience, sincerely reject". The target's stopping norm is such a standard as much as Exclusion is. An agent may sincerely reject either, and then it is, for her, a doubted norm.

Weatherson later states a parity outright: by the standard on which "Do the right thing" is not guiding, expected-rightness principles are not guiding either (2019, site p. 14). This supports parity, and only parity. It does not by itself establish Exclusion.

Sepielli (2009, pp. 8–9) separates conformity to a norm from following it, and discusses local rationality. These pages bear on the account of guidance through a weighing disposition or an acceptance, but they do not prove it, and the paper does not say they do. On guided action, see also Sepielli (2012, first proof, pp. 69–70).

None of this shows that Exclusion is free. "No loss of guidance" is one claim and "no cost" another, and the paper makes only the first.

Exclusion is a restriction of the enkratic conception, and it is not neutral. It may be objected that it declines to count a credence she holds, her credence in RR, and so gives up the enkratic commitment. The reply is a concession, not a denial: Exclusion does restrict the enkratic conception. But the departure is motivated. In a near-tie, weighing the reflexive reason as she takes it makes contrary beliefs about the verdict each true partly because held, which is the self-justification n. 25 condemns (§4); and a norm that counts a belief about its own verdict for the option the belief names is no standard for that belief.

The costs can be named. In a near-tie, an agent who credits RR and is confident that B is required may be required to do A. She may be required to break a promise keyed to what she believes rational, although the non-reflexive costs of breaking it still count. And the conceded limit on adviser opacity stands.

The first concession does not breach n. 27's principle that no rational norm can require an agent to choose an option she is "correctly certain" is irrational. Under Exclusion, her confidence that B is required is not correct, so the principle is not breached.

## 7. Conclusion

The result is what the target's defender must concede.

On the stop and its formulations: MEC as stated, and SD for A's requirement, make the verdict in a near-tie follow her belief about which option is required, with her first-order credences and her view of RR held fixed.

On independence: the conclusion at ms. p. 25, read broadly, fails for her. The qualified claim at ms. p. 18, of no direct sensitivity to beliefs about rationality, stands. So does n. 18 on the reading that holds every objective-reason credence fixed, though on that reading it cannot certify MEC's second-order standing for her. Retreating to either keeps the narrower claim only by giving up ms. p. 25.

On RR, which the target leaves open: the stop must count reflexive reasons as she takes them, count them at actual standing, or weigh none. The first ends in the self-justification n. 25 condemns. On the second, Exclusion withholds from a reflexive reason no verdict it could non-arbitrarily support. The third is Exclusion, a restriction of the enkratic conception, not neutral, with its costs named.

On fallibility: the Argument from Fallibility does not decide the case. The defender must concede that the near-tie self-confirmation is the self-justification n. 25 condemns, or supply a principled line, other than size or route, that keeps n. 25's charge.

The scope is two options, one reflexive reason, and MEC and SD. The paper does not claim that the formal fallibility argument entails Exclusion; that every agent faces a near-tie; that MEC lacks second-order standing, or the repaired norm a finite order, in Tarsney's sense; that what one rationally ought to do is independent of beliefs about rationality, which still count through the non-reflexive facts they cause or ground; or that Exclusion is neutral. Nor does it place the result against the wider literatures on the normativity of rationality, bootstrapping, enkrasia and higher-order evidence, or ratifiability.
