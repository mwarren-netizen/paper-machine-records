1. KEEP OR RESTORE THE PLAIN-LANGUAGE GLOSSES (A, B)
The line polish defines technical terms in passing. The structure polish does
not. In whichever text is used, define each at first use:
- "prior probability" (line polish: "its probability before the evidence is
  weighed");
- "credence" (line polish: "or degree of confidence");
- the prior ratio P(G)/P(M) and the likelihood ratio P(E|G)/P(E|M) (line
  polish: "the ratio of the hypotheses' probabilities before the evidence";
  "the ratio of the probabilities each hypothesis assigns to the evidence");
- "posterior ratios" (line polish: "ratios of probabilities after the evidence
  is weighed");
- Symes's distinction (line polish: "absurdity before the evidence is weighed
  (a priori) from absurdity in light of it (a posteriori)"; structure polish:
  "separated a priori from a posteriori absurdity");
- "perfectly free" (line polish: "moved only by its knowledge of what is
  good");
- "metaethics" (line polish: "to questions about value and motivation").

2. SAY WHAT THE SIMPLICITY REPLY IS, AND WHAT SWINBURNE'S MEASURE COUNTS AS
SIMPLER (A, B)
(a) Introduction (B). The line polish explains the reply: "God is perfectly
free: moved only by its knowledge of what is good, it needs no further motive.
A being that does evil needs a desire to do it. Simpler hypotheses are more
probable, so God is more probable than the evil god." The structure polish
cuts this. The reader is told that "the most developed reply appeals to
simplicity" but not, until Section 2, why simplicity favours God. The
introduction's later claims (the cap, "Swinburne's premise about value and
motivation") depend on it. Keep or restore a brief statement of the reply.
(b) Swinburne's measure (A).
- The paper relies on Swinburne's account of simplicity throughout, but the
  reader meets it only in fragments:
  - fewer properties ("the absence of properties, such as non-rational
    desires");
  - infinite values simpler than finite ones;
  - a penalty on desires a being "variously has".
- By the simplicity section, the argument turns on what the measure favours
  ("Swinburne's measure favours extremes"; "the cheap setting").
- Somewhere early, say in two or three sentences what the account counts as
  simpler and why simplicity is supposed to raise prior probability. Use the
  paper's own words, from the sources it already cites.
- The general lesson from paper-2-r2: when a paper borrows a framework from
  elsewhere and leans on it, a newcomer needs a short account of what the
  framework is before its details are used.

3. SAY WHY THE CAP HOLDS, IN THE INTRODUCTION (B)
The line polish gives the reason that simplicity's advantage is capped: "For
if that premise is false, and nothing else about value favours the good, a
good god needs a motive as much as an evil one does." The structure polish
states the cap with no reason ("the advantage simplicity can supply is capped
by the theist's confidence in Swinburne's premise about value and
motivation"). Give the reader the one-sentence reason, and name the premise
as the line polish does ("that a perfectly free being who knows what is good
will do it").

4. GLOSS THE BOUND WHERE IT APPEARS (A; already done in the structure polish)
In the line polish, the display k.r >= t/epsilon is followed by a long
paragraph on Page and Baker-Hytch and the rough symmetry. Only then comes the
plain-words reading ("This requirement sets a lower bound... At one chance in
a million..."). Put the trade-off straight after the display, so the formula
is read in words while it is still in view. The structure polish already does
this (item 0(a)).

5. TRANSLATE THE MATHEMATICS INTO WORDS (A, B)
The lesson from paper-5's revision: where a formula or condition matters to
the argument, give a rough statement of what it means in plain words. The
paper already does this in places, and those are the model:
- after the cap, "the advantage simplicity gives God is at most about n times
  the odds on Swinburne's premise";
- the conclusion's "the theist's credence in God is her credence in the evil
  god multiplied by God's combined advantage".
Reviewer B recommends that most of the spare words go here. The passages that
need it:
(a) The two-case argument ("Where the Evil God's Improbability Lies", or the
    second half of line §3), from "Let d be the factor by which the total
    evidence changes the evil god's probability" through "so nearly all of
    the evil god's improbability lies in its prior" (B). Say in words:
    - what d measures;
    - why P(G|E) <= r.d;
    - what each case amounts to;
    - why the two cases exhaust the possibilities.
    For the second case in particular (A): "Then P(M) = epsilon/d <=
    r.epsilon/t, so nearly all of the evil god's improbability lies in its
    prior." The step to "nearly all" is left to the reader. With the paper's
    own values (r = 1, t = 1/2), it says that the evil god's prior is at most
    twice its final credence. An evil god at one in a million therefore
    starts out at no more than two in a million: the evidence has done almost
    nothing, and the prior has done the rest.
(b) The derivation of the cap (A, B): "Then P(G | S), which cannot exceed the
    probability of a creator, is at most about n times P(G | not-S)." This is
    the step on which the cap rests, and its reason is compressed into a
    relative clause. Spelled out:
    - given not-S, a creator's motive is one of n equally likely simplest
      motives, so God gets about a 1/n share of the creator's probability;
    - given S, God can get at most all of it;
    - since S does not raise the creator's probability, the second is at most
      n times the first.
    Say this, and briefly why each later step holds ("Since P(M) >=
    P(not-S).P(M | not-S), and P(G) = ..."), before the formula k <= 1 +
    n.P(S)/P(not-S).
(c) "A credence of at least t/(rk)" (B): say what this is.

6. REMIND READERS WHAT THE SYMBOLS AND THE BOUND MEAN (A, B)
- The paper uses k, r, epsilon, t, d, S and n. Epsilon is not a small
  quantity in general but the evil god's final (posterior) credence, which a
  reader may not expect (B).
- When k.r >= t/epsilon returns, say in a few words what each symbol stands
  for (B). It returns in "What a Gap Solution Must Supply", in the simplicity
  section ("a prior advantage for God of at least t/(r epsilon)") and in the
  conclusion.
- "The bound" is coined in "How Large the Advantage Must Be" ("sets a lower
  bound") and used later without restatement (A). "What a Gap Solution Must
  Supply" opens with "The bound shows this to be mistaken"; restate the bound
  in words there.

7. ONE NAME FOR EACH CHALLENGE (A, B)
- The two versions of the challenge have two names each:
  - "comparative" and "absolute" (abstract, introduction, conclusion);
  - "Challenge I" and "Challenge II" (Section 2 and after).
- The mapping is given once, in Section 2 ("Challenge I is comparative ...
  Challenge II is absolute"). A reader who reaches "It answers Challenge II
  only if..." has to remember which is which.
- Either use one pair throughout, keeping Mooney and Hendricks's numerals only
  in quotation, or tie the labels together at each return (for example,
  "Challenge II, the absolute challenge").

8. ONE NAME FOR THE EVIL-GOD HYPOTHESIS, AND FOR THE SYMMETRY PREMISE (A)
(a) The evil-god hypothesis appears as "the evil god", "maltheism" (Mooney and
Hendricks), M, "diabolism" (Collins), "EGH" (Lancaster-Thomas) and "EG" (Page
and Baker-Hytch). God likewise appears as "theism", G, "GGH" and "GG". Each is
glossed where it appears, which is right, but that is five names for one
hypothesis. Say once, early, that the paper calls it "the evil god"
(formally M) and that the authors quoted use other labels. Each quoted label
then needs only "(their name for the evil god)".
(b) The premise the whole argument is conditional on has four descriptions:
- "its central premise, that the evidence fits the two gods about equally
  well" (introduction);
- "the two symmetries" (Section 2, where the two grounds of Challenge I have
  not yet been called symmetries);
- "the symmetry premise" (the bound section);
- "the symmetry of likelihoods" (conclusion).
Name it at first use and keep the name. In Section 2, say that the two
grounds just given are the "two symmetries" the quotation goes on to mention.

9. LABELS THAT RETURN AFTER A GAP, AND TERMS USED UNGLOSSED (A)
- "The first option" and "the second option" first appear in "What a Gap

  the choice ("She may hold ... Or she may give...") but doesn't number the

10. WORDING SLIPS AND PRECISION (B, with A on "exactly")
(a) Slips in the structure polish (B):
- "under symmetry the evidence lowers God exactly as it lowers the evil god.
  To say that it lowers the evil god by more than half is to say that it
  lowers God by more than half". As written, the evidence lowers God; it
  should lower God's *probability*, as in the line polish.
- "evidence that fits both gods equally moves them together" is vaguer than
  the line polish's "changes their probabilities by the same factor".
- "His second is right of the difference between the two probabilities ...
  but not of their ratio": "right of" is unidiomatic. The line polish has
  "right about".
- "reaches the evil god only if the evil god is among the many" and "The evil
  god belongs to a small class of simplest motives, not to the many": "the
  many" is undefined. The line polish says "one of the many rivals among
  which the remaining probability is spread thinly".
- "since such knowledge might incline without compelling" is compressed. The
  line polish says "might incline a being to do good without compelling it to
  do so".
- "Now exactly." and "Start with the shape of the point." are abrupt
  transitions.
(b) "Exactly" overstates the symmetry (A). Both polishes say the evidence
lowers God's probability by "exactly" the evil god's factor. The paper's
premise, however, is that r is "close to one", and it allows the symmetry to
be "only rough". "Exactly" holds only at r = 1. Combined fix: "lowers God's
probability by about the same factor (exactly, when r = 1)". The argument of
the next sentence needs only rough equality.
(c) Smaller (A). "God's credence is at most k.r times the evil god's" (in the
Miller discussion) is true but weaker than the paper's own equation,
P(G|E) = k.r.epsilon exactly. Check that "at most" is the intended claim.
These are precision points, not changes to the argument.

11. THE PAPER'S OWN MOVE IN THE SIMPLICITY SECTION NEEDS SLOWING DOWN (A, B)
- The argument that the evil god is among the simplest rivals is the paper's
  most original step. It begins with "Swinburne's measure favours extremes";
  then what privileges malevolence is that "its object is supplied by the
  facts that make God good".
- It carries the cap: without it, the argument from the continuum would sink
  the evil god, and the cap would have nothing to cap.
- It is dense:
  - "fixed by the same facts with the direction reversed";
  - "needs no content beyond them";
  - Draper's penalty "spares a uniform anti-good motive";
  - strength "set at the limit".
- Check that a reader new to Swinburne can follow each step:
  - why limitlessness is cheap on Swinburne's measure;
  - why a reversed valuation needs no further content, while a desire for
    order does;
  - why Draper's penalty spares it;
  - why the egoist god is the stated limit.
- The paper's own contrast between the evil god and a god with a maximal
  desire for order could be made a plain comparison of what each hypothesis
  has to specify (A).
- Keep every qualification at its present strength (A, B).

12. SAY WHAT EACH SECTION OR PASSAGE IS FOR (A, B)
(a) The Law paragraph (A). The paragraph on Law's elves of Swindon closes the
bound section (line §3 / structure §3), after the symmetry discussion, and
reads as an afterthought. It does real work:
- the conclusion counts its result among what the paper establishes ("Law's
  point about the elves of Swindon is best read as a claim about absolute
  credence");
- the plan gave it a section of its own ("What Law should have said").
Open the paragraph with a sentence saying what it does: it corrects the
challenger's own reply to simplicity, and the correction is the bound applied
to Law's example.
(b) The survey in "What a Gap Solution Must Supply" (B).
- The section runs through Swinburne, Miller (2016), Collins, Symes,
  Lancaster-Thomas and Miller (2021) in quick succession. Make sure the reader
  knows why each is there: each either fails to state a size, or (Miller 2021)
  states one and so takes the second option.
- A short sentence at the start saying what the survey is looking for would
  help. "None of the solutions in print that I examined states a prior
  ratio..." does this, but it comes after the objection-and-reply paragraphs;
  consider whether it should come first.
- The "by definition" objection and its reply are dense. Check that the
  distinction between the two readings of "successful" is clear.
(c) General (A). Check that each section's job is visible when a reader
enters it.

14. CONCRETE ILLUSTRATIONS (B; A optional)
(a) The price of the second option is a strong example (B). "A theist with
k = 10, r = 1 and a credence in God of 0.8 must give the evil god 0.08: one
chance in twelve that the world was made by an omnipotent being who wants us
to suffer" is the paper's most vivid illustration. It comes late. Consider
whether the introduction could point to it, as it already does with the
one-in-a-million figure. Claim no more than the paper does.
(b) One illustrative theist, carried through (A, optional). The worked numbers
change from section to section:
- one in a million;
- one in a thousand and 0.3;
- a prior ratio of a hundred;
- k = 10 and 50;
- n = 3 and one per cent.
Each set is clear on its own, but the reader can't watch one theist's
position change as the argument proceeds. Consider fixing one illustrative
theist early (say, an evil god at one in a million, belief at one half,
r = 1) and returning to her at each stage:
- the bound she faces;
- what the solutions in print give her;
- what simplicity can give her on the model, and what she must therefore
  believe about Swinburne's premise.
The pieces are already in the paper; this changes how they are threaded, not
what they show. Mind the budget: (a) is the cheaper way to get much of the
same effect.
