The candidate, *Can a Deontologist Escape Paralysis by Living for the Long Term?*, is clearly stronger than *Reference B*, somewhat stronger than *Reference A*, and a close competitor to *Reference C*. I give it a modest advantage over the Reference C paper in demonstrated argumentative support, while finding the latter’s conceptual payoff more distinctive. These judgments concern achievement within the papers’ respective scopes, rather than their subjects’ importance.

The candidate’s central achievement is identifying why two reasons concerning the same distant future need not balance. On the paralysis premises, an identity-affecting longtermist life incurs the doing-harm premium across the baseline future, whereas its improvement depends on the agent’s marginal influence. Section 3 isolates these quantities as (k−1)PH and Δ(B−kH), yielding the condition r > k+(k−1)/ε. The algebra makes a substantive philosophical point: merely relocating beneficence to the distant future does not remove the burden that allegedly prohibits ordinary action. Relative to the D’Alessandro argument as presented here, locating the cost in the replaced future rather than only the additional future provides genuine intellectual leverage.

Sections 4–5 strengthen this contribution. A premium on doing good can undermine paralysis itself rather than vindicate a distinctive longtermist escape; restricting the harm premium’s reach reverses the role of future size. These are explanatory distinctions, not ornamental complications. The limitations matter, however. The asymmetries alone do not entail constant additive weights, and the paper explicitly acknowledges this in section 3. Its donor estimate derives from a quoted lower bound on effectiveness, while its road-accident calculation cannot constrain an advanced future’s benefit–harm ratio. The paper recognizes both points, so its exact conditional results are considerably firmer than any general practical verdict. Leaving the defensibility of a remoteness limit open does not undermine the diagnosis; it does prevent treating the proposed escape as defended.

This combination of explicit assumptions and delivered consequences puts the candidate well ahead of *Reference B*. That paper usefully distinguishes “refuting equivocation” from “justifying validity” and transfers the distinction from morality to modality. Its shift from desires to commitments is also motivated: practical inconsistency need not resemble conflicting appetites. But the crucial inferential machinery receives insufficient justification. Moving between disapproval and approval of that disapproval requires the proposed evaluative axioms; describing coordination as requiring coherence does not establish those particular higher-order transitions or the conditional’s logical behavior. The best response supplied is that commitments, unlike wishes, have goals frustrated by inconsistency. That supports a norm of consistency without yet vindicating the formal deductions. The modest conclusion—“a gesture to how it must be solved”—appropriately limits ambition, but leaves even the illustrated solution less secure than the candidate’s conditional analysis.

*Reference A* achieves more than this blueprint. Its progression from conative variation through individual and communal inferential variation explains why simply replacing attitudes with inferential roles cannot solve univocity. Thrasymachus and the Levitical community make the distinction between individual deviation and divergent communal standards intelligible. The final proposal also has substantive content: synonymy judgments can constitute endorsements of shared inferential accountability, rather than report independently fixed semantic identities. This is a fruitful framework, and its achievement does not depend on supplying a complete theory of moral discourse.

Nevertheless, its claim to have “defanged” Merli’s strategy is less fully secured than the candidate’s diagnosis. Even granting the constitutive account, the usefulness of constructing a communicative bridge needs differentiation from reasons to regard an existing dispute as univocal. Shared coordination aims can accompany different concepts, while genuine disagreement can resist fruitful negotiation. Section 5’s alien-discourse example and the final anthropological footnote acknowledge contextual limits; they support a flexible interpretive practice, but do not themselves resolve this pressure. The candidate more clearly establishes which conclusions follow from its commitments. That warrants a qualified comparative advantage, without dismissing the inferential framework because it remains developable.

The Reference C paper presents the closest match. Its use of expressivism about correct linguistic rules gives quasi-realism a philosophically productive application: denying mind-dependence without restoring materialist explanatory foundations. Sections 4–5 then distinguish a theory’s truth conditions from the beliefs produced by attempting to endorse it. The account of dynamic endorsement is illuminating; note 55 importantly restricts the unbelievability claim to full, explicit belief. These moves deliver considerably more than a juxtaposition of familiar positions.

Its vulnerability lies in extending the expressive strategy to the opponent’s claims about that very strategy. The proposed reading of “Reality determines which linguistic rules are correct” as approval of responding to subjective regularities does not visibly break the symmetry between rival rule-followers. The paper recognizes this and continues to higher-order reinterpretations. That yields an interesting conditional account of self-effacement, but accepting each further reinterpretation carries more argumentative weight than demonstrating the initial transfer. Its explicit assumption that quasi-realist moves succeed deserves respect, just as the candidate’s model assumptions do; it nevertheless leaves greater uncertainty about the construction’s coherence. The candidate’s narrower result is more firmly established, while Reference C offers the more adventurous explanatory possibility. These texts support judgments of apparent distinctiveness, not verified originality.
