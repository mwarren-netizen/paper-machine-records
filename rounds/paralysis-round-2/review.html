What the argument establishes

The main thesis is intelligible without the equations: a longtermist does not escape an identity-based constraint merely by aiming at the distant future, because her action changes the identities of people in the future that would probably have existed anyway. The deontological burden can therefore attach to much more than the beneficial change she produces.

Here is the argument independently reconstructed:

  1. Accept, for the argument, that active lives produce remote identity effects classified as doings, while stillness merely allows corresponding outcomes.
  2. Represent benefits and harms by common conditional totals B and H, and the baseline probability of a long future by P. Give harms done weight k, harms allowed weight 1, and initially benefits either way weight 1.
  3. A longtermist life raises survival probability by Δ, but changes identities throughout the modeled future on survival branches, including the baseline P branches.
  4. Its excess choice-worthiness over stillness is consequently Δ(B−kH)−(k−1)PH. The second term remains even when Δ is tiny.
  5. Thus the escape requires B/H > k+(k−1)/(Δ/P). A large population alone does not improve this ratio.
  6. Giving benefits done a premium creates two distinct thresholds: one for longtermism beating stillness, another for ordinary life beating stillness. For small individual margins and the illustrative weights, the specifically longtermist escape occupies a narrow interval.
  7. Limiting the harm premium's reach reduces the baseline burden without necessarily reducing the whole long-run benefit. This explains how future size can help under a restricted premium although it does not help under the unrestricted model.

I independently checked the threshold algebra, the relative-band expression and numerical examples, the trajectory inequality, and the horizon inequality. They are correct on the stated strict comparisons. Equality cases can simply be described as ties. Permissibility claims should be understood within the compared options and the assumed choice-worthiness rule; the calculations do not independently eliminate prerogatives or other permissible options.

The strongest established conclusion is conditional: this familiar-looking additive realization of the paralysis reasoning does not support the proposed escape merely from a long-term orientation, and it identifies the quantities that determine success. It does not prove that all deontology prohibits longtermist action, that actual individual influence has an upper bound, or that remote harms really are the agent's doings.

Strongest objection and the author's best reply

The strongest objection is that the model adds substantive commitments to the target's asymmetries: constant interpersonal additive weights, common conditional benefit–harm totals, and effectively complete identity replacement over the modeled future. A critic can accept asymmetric reasons while rejecting that representation. Even an interval-scale ranking of outcomes does not entail linear aggregation of every unit of harm.

The paper's explicit diagnostic-model paragraph is a good response. It does not pretend to derive additivity from the asymmetries. Its conditional counterexample is enough to defeat the claim that orientation toward the long term itself supplies the missing justification. The observation that baseline identity effects survive a small beneficial margin also remains philosophically illuminating beyond the exact formula. But the abstract's “on their premises” should not sound as though the exact thresholds follow from those premises alone. Add one short clause identifying common conditional totals and full spread of identity effects as idealizations. Do not develop a general representation theorem or add another model.

There are two further local overclaims. The intended-benefit paragraph does not establish why the premium must apply only to the marginal survival improvement. And section 5 moves too quickly from an agent's neither doing nor allowing an outcome to its having no reason-giving value. Neither passage is needed for the principal result. Their smallest adequate repairs are identified below.

Significance and originality

The contribution is a correction to a real argumentative transition: having reasons concerning the same temporal domain does not ensure that an agent's improvement can outweigh the cost attributed to the whole affected domain. The result is neither a discovery that longtermism can be demanding nor a generic observation that good futures can contain suffering. Sections 4 and 5 explain respectively when an escape collapses into an independent rejection of paralysis, and why the reach of an agent-relative premium matters separately from temporal discounting.

Concede everything: a defender of the escape must establish adequate improvement relative to baseline identity effects, or revise how the premium applies. Appealing only to astronomical future size and devotion to posterity is insufficient. That is a material constraint on the proposed response, although it need not persuade anyone who rejects the original paralysis premises.

Perfect prose: yes, the central contribution would still merit submission. Its value lies in identifying a neglected cost and organizing the alternatives, not in its notation. The narrow-band result and the reversal under a restricted premium add enough understanding to justify the additional machinery.

The baseline/margin mechanism and the joint threshold analysis appear distinctive among the directly consulted antecedents; I did not find an exact predecessor in the focused searches. This is a bounded novelty judgment, not a certification of originality across the literature. [unchecked]

Structure, prose, and economy

The reverse outline is:

  1. Introduction: establish the proposed escape, state baseline-versus-margin diagnosis, announce model and scope.
  2. Paralysis and escape: reconstruct the precise target and its missing comparison.
  3. Cost: derive the basic threshold; illustrate scale; address trajectory improvement and antecedents.
  4. Benefit premium: distinguish persistence of paralysis, a specifically longtermist escape, and independent defeat of paralysis.
  5. Restricted reach: derive the contrasting mechanism; examine whether cited accounts supply it.
  6. Deontic longtermism: explain the conditional relevance to the side-constraint premise.
  7. Conclusion: restate the result and open questions.

This order works. The question and answer precede the equations; the additional distinctions arise where they do argumentative work. Section 6 is a useful implication, provided it remains explicitly conditional rather than alleging inconsistency between authors' commitments. No replacement outline is warranted.

The prose is generally direct and readable. The abstract and introduction state a genuine puzzle and the answer. Repeated explanations of “whole cost, small gain” can be shortened, but they mostly orient the reader rather than conceal a missing argument. Three passages deserve substantive local changes:

  1. Section 4: “Such a premium touches only the margin Δ.” This is asserted rather than argued. Intending an improved chance of survival is not obviously equivalent to intending benefits only on the additional survival branches. A clearer bounded formulation is: “If the intention premium attaches only to the additional expected benefit, it leaves the baseline harm premium intact.” Alternatively delete this paragraph; the paper need not settle the theory of intended benefits.

  2. Section 5: “If remoteness removed the agent from remote outcomes altogether ... the long-run gain ... would lapse with them.” Exclusion from doing/allowing classifications does not alone entail exclusion of impersonal reasons to promote good. Replace the categorical necessity claim with: “The present escape preserves the reasons supplied by remote benefits while reducing the additional weight attached to remote harms. A view that excluded those benefits from practical assessment would not supply this escape.” This preserves the useful distinction without claiming that every other agency-based account must fail.

  3. Section 3: “his argument turns on weighting bad lives above good ones.” Replace with a description of the actual distinction: “Mogensen's earlier challenge concerns whether improvements involving different future people generate requiring reasons, rather than only justifying reasons.” The current compression risks both misdescribing an antecedent and confusing a population-ethical asymmetry with the premium analyzed here. [contradicted: Mogensen 2019, Non-Requiring View discussion, PDF pp. 21–27]

These are local argumentative and literature corrections, not a diagnosis of generally poor prose. The abstract's effectiveness sentence could additionally say “Using their conservative illustrative figures” to make the numerical status immediately clear.

A whitespace count of the supplied Markdown gives 5,383 words including metadata, abstract, notes and references. This is not a journal's definitive count, but it places the draft close to the 5,500-word limit. Delete the intended-benefit detour and shorten repeated summaries to fund the scope clause and accurate antecedent description. No expansion is necessary.

Three priority interventions and stopping point

P1 — Bound the ancillary exclusion claims. Correct or remove the intended-benefit paragraph and the categorical agency/value inference. They overstate what has been established and offer avoidable referee targets. Adequate repair is conditional wording or deletion, leaving the principal derivations unchanged. This is a small repair, not a new literature project.

P2 — Correct the antecedent and record verification honestly. Replace the Mogensen sentence and check the outstanding quotation/page references against the author's source copies during ordinary submission preparation. Accurate positioning matters, but no current finding requires abandoning the baseline/margin contribution. Adequate repair is a faithful short description and a confirmed or removed quotation, not a comprehensive new survey. [contradicted: Mogensen 2019, Non-Requiring View discussion, PDF pp. 21–27]

P3 — Align the prominent claim with the model's scope. Add the common-distribution/full-spread idealizations to the assumptions paragraph and ensure the abstract advertises a diagnostic conditional result. Keep the floor/illustration qualifications close to the numerical claim. Adequate repair lets a reader identify the precise conclusion without mistaking it for a theorem about all asymmetry-respecting deontology.

The most promising paper inside this one is already the one it substantially delivers: a diagnosis of how the reach of agent-relative burdens interacts with marginal improvements. A broader positive account of remoteness or collective agency would be a separate project with uncertain prospects; I have not checked its originality. The main problem is a few claims outrunning an otherwise successful conditional analysis, together with one misleading antecedent description. Correct those locally and stop revising. The calibrated standard does not call for reconstruction, reconception, or abandonment.
